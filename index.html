Warm-up: The Preference Heist 🕵️

A team deduction game: choose the most useful response and explain why.

How to play
  1. Split into two teams. Take turns choosing an answer.
  2. One point for a correct answer; a bonus point for adding a follow-up question.
  3. Everyone must help their team decide.

Preference Lab 💬

Goal: ask a partner about free-time activities, understand their answer, and find one thing you have in common.

Why are we doing this?In the warm-up you chose responses. Now we’ll learn the exact phrases that help us ask about hobbies and answer naturally. First read the model, then practise choosing the right phrase, and finally use the phrases with a partner.
See one short example first

A: Do you like listening to music?

B: Yes, I do. I listen to music every day.

A: Me too! What kind of music do you like?

B: I like pop music.

Notice: the first question checks a preference; the follow-up question gets more information.

Pair mission: Two questions, one connection

Work with one partner. Student A asks both questions below; Student B answers in a complete sentence. Then switch roles.

1. Do you prefer music or podcasts?
2. What do you like doing in your free time?

After both people answer, find one thing you both like — or one difference.

Be ready to tell the class one sentence. You do not need to interview three classmates or count votes.

Why listening comes nextWe can now ask about activities and preferences. In the next task, listen for how people share information in real situations. Your job is not to understand every word; it is to catch the main situation and a few details you can explain to another group.

Three-pass strategy
  1. First time: understand the general situation. Don't write every word.
  2. Second time: note the important details and one useful expression.
  3. Mixed pairs: each expert explains their resource; the listener asks a follow-up question.

Why imperatives?While listening, you may hear people ask someone to do something or give an instruction. These short verb forms are called imperatives. We’ll practise them now so you can recognise and use them in everyday conversations.
4

Quick Commands ⚡

Imperatives help us give instructions, make requests and warn someone.

Mini grammar cards

Positive

Base verb: Listen to this!
Tell me the name.

Negative

Don't + base verb
Don't forget the time.
Don't buy that album.

Polite

Please can go at either end:
Please tell me again.
Tell me again, please.

Why recommendations?The lesson uses imperatives such as “Listen to this!” and “Don't buy it.” The same forms help us recommend something or warn a friend. In the next challenge, you’ll use a command plus a reason, such as “Try this app. It's useful.”

Conversation Studio 🎤

Finish and perform the dialogue you didn't have time to complete in Class 3.

Your task
  1. Work with a partner. Use your “My World of Interests” writing from last class.
  2. Create a natural conversation, not a list of unrelated questions.
  3. Include at least six questions, detailed answers, one comparison, and one recommendation with a reason.
  4. Practise before presenting. Both partners should speak.
Conversation building blocks

Open

Model conversation — use it as a guide, not a script to copy

Alex: Hi, Sam! What do you like doing in your free time?

Sam: I like playing video games and listening to music. What about you?

Alex: I prefer watching movies. Do you like action movies?

Sam: Not really. I prefer comedies because they're funny. What kind of music do you like?

Alex: I love pop music. Do you listen to it?

Sam: Sometimes. I usually listen to music when I do homework.

Alex: You should try this new playlist. It's great!

Sam: Cool! What's it called?

Alex: “Weekend Mix.” Search for it after class.

Sam: Thanks! And you should watch my favourite comedy sometime.

Alex: Sounds good. See you later!

Notice: the speakers ask follow-up questions, answer with details, compare preferences, and give recommendations.
